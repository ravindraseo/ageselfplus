The source

Why Japanese Knotweed and Not Red Wine?

Resveratrol shot to fame through the "French paradox" — the idea that some wine-loving populations seemed to have surprisingly healthy hearts. Here's the part that rarely makes the headlines: a glass of red wine holds only a few milligrams of resveratrol at most. Reaching 125 mg from wine simply isn't realistic, or wise.

Japanese knotweed is a hardy perennial plant whose roots are among the richest natural sources of resveratrol known. It has a long history in traditional East Asian herbal practice, and today it's the raw material behind most resveratrol supplements because it lets a meaningful, consistent amount fit inside one small capsule.

Put simply: knotweed gives you the compound people are curious about — without the alcohol, sugar or calories that come with wine.

Resveratrol 101

What Is Resveratrol? A Plain-English Primer

Before deciding whether AGE+ is worth your money, it helps to understand the one compound it's built around. Resveratrol is a polyphenol — a natural chemical that certain plants produce when they're under pressure from things like fungal infection, injury or strong ultraviolet light. Think of it as part of the plant's own defense kit.

Trans-resveratrol vs. cis-resveratrol

Resveratrol exists in two shapes, called isomers. Trans-resveratrol is the more stable, more common form and the one used in the majority of human studies. Cis-resveratrol forms when the trans version is exposed to light and heat, and it has received far less research attention. That's why serious labels — including AGE+ — specify "trans-resveratrol" instead of just "resveratrol." If a product doesn't say which form it contains, that's a small but useful warning sign.

Where else is resveratrol found?

You already eat small amounts of it. Red and purple grape skins, blueberries, cranberries, mulberries, peanuts, pistachios and dark chocolate all contain traces. The amounts, though, are tiny — usually well under a milligram per serving. Enjoy these foods for their overall nutrition, fiber and flavor; just don't expect them to deliver anything close to the 125 mg found in one AGE+ capsule.

The absorption question

One of the most discussed topics among researchers is bioavailability — how much of the resveratrol you swallow actually circulates in your bloodstream. The body absorbs resveratrol reasonably well but processes it very quickly in the gut and liver. This is one reason people often take resveratrol with a meal containing some fat, and why consistent daily use tends to make more sense than occasional, irregular doses. It's also a good reminder to be skeptical of any brand claiming "instant" effects.

The research

What Scientists Have Actually Studied

Resveratrol is one of the most researched plant polyphenols. Still, general resveratrol research is not research on the finished AGE+ product, and human results are mixed.

Read the Linus Pauling Institute overview →
01

Oxidative stress

Early interest came from resveratrol's antioxidant activity in the lab. Researchers have looked at how it interacts with the body's own defenses against oxidative stress — something that naturally rises with age, poor sleep and environmental exposure.

02

Sirtuins & cellular maintenance

Resveratrol became famous for its studied link with sirtuins, proteins involved in cellular upkeep. Most of that evidence comes from yeast, worms and animals; whether the same happens in people taking a capsule is still an open question.

03

Metabolism & glucose markers

Several human trials have measured insulin sensitivity and blood-sugar markers. Some saw modest changes, others saw none — dose, duration and who was studied seem to matter a lot.

04

Heart & blood-vessel function

Studies have looked at blood-vessel flexibility and circulation markers. Some findings are encouraging, but they aren't consistent enough for firm conclusions.

The honest bottom line: resveratrol is a genuinely interesting compound, but no supplement — AGE+ included — has been shown to stop aging, extend human lifespan or prevent disease. Want to dig in yourself? Browse current studies on PubMed.

Safety first

SelfPlus AGE+ Side Effects, Safety & Interactions

This is the section most reviews skip, and it's the one we think matters most. Resveratrol at moderate amounts, like the 125 mg in AGE+, is generally well tolerated by healthy adults. That said, "natural" never automatically means "right for everyone," so here's what to know before your first capsule.

Possible mild side effects

The most commonly reported complaints with resveratrol supplements are digestive: a slightly upset stomach, loose stools or mild nausea. These are more often linked with much higher intakes than the amount in AGE+, and taking the capsule with food usually helps. If anything feels off and doesn't settle within a few days, stop and talk to a healthcare professional.

Medication interactions to take seriously

  • Blood thinners and antiplatelet drugsResveratrol may have mild blood-thinning properties, so combining it with warfarin, clopidogrel or daily aspirin deserves a conversation with your doctor.
  • Medicines processed by liver enzymesResveratrol can affect certain liver enzymes that break down many common prescriptions. Your pharmacist can check your list in a couple of minutes.
  • Hormone-sensitive conditionsResveratrol has weak plant-estrogen activity, so anyone with a hormone-sensitive condition should get medical advice first.
  • Upcoming surgeryMany doctors suggest stopping supplements that may affect bleeding about two weeks before a procedure.
Simple rule of thumb: if you take any prescription medicine, show the AGE+ label to your doctor or pharmacist before starting. It's a five-minute check that gives real peace of mind.
